# Social Engineering Principles

Social engineering exploits **human psychology** rather than technical vulnerabilities. These principles apply to phishing, vishing, pretexting, physical access attacks — any scenario where the target is a person.

---

## The Six Principles

### Scarcity

Makes something feel rare so people act before thinking. FOMO (fear of missing out) and loss aversion kick in — we hate losing a chance more than we enjoy gaining something.

**Signals:** "limited seats", "last chance", "only 3 left", "ends today"

### Urgency

Adds a countdown. Time pressure narrows attention and reduces careful checking, especially when the consequence sounds inconvenient (lockout, suspension, missed deadline).

**Signals:** "within 24 hours", "immediately", "deadline passed", "act now"

> **Scarcity vs Urgency:** Scarcity = limited quantity ("only 3 left"). Urgency = limited time ("expires in 24 hours"). Often combined for maximum pressure.

### Authority

Leans on perceived status or expertise for quick compliance. People follow instructions from leaders, experts, or official departments without questioning. Visual cues (titles, signatures, logos, formal tone) amplify the effect.

**Signals:** "From: IT Administrator", "As per the CEO's request", "HR Department notice"

### Fear

Uses threat and alarm to trigger a protective reaction. Anxiety overrides skepticism when the risk sounds personal — account compromise, legal trouble, financial loss.

**Signals:** "security alert", "breach detected", "unauthorized access", "your account has been compromised"

### Curiosity

Hooks attention by promising interesting or exclusive information. The brain wants to close information gaps, which can outweigh caution. Subject lines are short, intriguing, and slightly vague.

**Signals:** "Confidential:", "You won't believe...", "Exclusive access", "See who viewed your profile"

### Trust

Piggybacks on familiar brands, colleagues, or communication styles so the message feels safe by default. Recognizable names, logos, or routines (monthly reports, ticket numbers) lower skepticism.

**Signals:** Microsoft logo, company email template, "As discussed in yesterday's meeting", familiar sender name

---

## Cognitive Biases

Beyond the six principles, attackers exploit built-in thinking shortcuts:

| Bias | What Happens | Exploitation |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| **Overconfidence** | "I'm too smart to fall for this" | Leads to less vigilance when checking suspicious messages |
| **Confirmation** | Accept info that fits expectations | Expecting a bank email? Trust the phishing one that looks like it |
| **Authority** | Trust messages from authority figures without question | Email from "the CEO" gets acted on immediately |
